A decade of Texas pharmacy churn, from a roster the state keeps deleting (2014-2026)

The Texas State Board of Pharmacy publishes one file of every licensed pharmacy site, rebuilds it every day, and keeps no history. The Internet Archive happened to capture that file 49 distinct times between November 2014 and July 2025 - the only public history of any US state's pharmacy roster. We replayed all of it, then joined it to the live daily record this site keeps. This is what twelve years of a roster look like when you stop letting it be overwritten.

The single most important number: 92.7%. Of the 5,871 licenses that vanished from the roster across twelve years, 5,437 were still marked "Active" at their final appearance. The board never published any terminal status for them; they simply ceased to exist. The remaining 7.3% briefly showed Delinquent (208), Expired (185), Probation (27), Revocation (4) or Suspension (3) before being purged. Anyone building "pharmacy closure" statistics from the board's own current file, or from registries that inherit from it, is reading a record with the closures deleted.

Headline findings

  • 5,871 Texas pharmacy licenses vanished since November 2014 - equivalent to 74% of the state's 2014 roster - while the total grew from 7,904 to 8,592 licensed sites. Steady state is roughly 480 removals and 530 new licenses a year on a base of ~8,000: about 6% of all licensed pharmacies disappear every year.
  • Removal is permanent. Of 5,871 licenses that vanished, 7 ever reappeared (0.12%). A license leaving the roster is a terminal event with 99.9% reliability - which is why a recorder can call closures from one confirming capture.
  • Four rural counties lost their only licensed pharmacy and never got one back: Hall, Jim Hogg, Lipscomb and Marion counties each had exactly one in 2014 and have zero today. (County names were normalized first - the board changed its own spellings mid-series, e.g. "MC LENNAN" → "MCLENNAN"; a naive diff invents fake deserts.)
  • The suburban shift, in one pair of numbers: Harris County (Houston) is down 132 in-state licenses since 2014; Fort Bend County, Houston's south-western suburbs, is up exactly 132. Collin County (Dallas suburbs) is up 129 while Dallas County is down 41.
  • Rename waves mark chain consolidation: 4,226 name changes cluster in 2016 (883), 2021 (778) and 2023 (522) - rebrand and ownership-transfer events visible nowhere else at this granularity.
  • Mail-order share is flat. Out-of-state (mail-order / specialty) licensure held at ~10.6% of the roster across the whole decade - against the common narrative of runaway mail-order growth, at least in licensure terms.
  • Chain vs independent: the stock is stable, the flow is wildly asymmetric. Among in-state community pharmacies (name-classified), chain share moved only 39.6% → 39.9% since 2014 - but 43.8% of every independent pharmacy ever seen has vanished, versus 20.3% of chain sites (2.2x). The stable share is explained by independents also forming at a far higher rate: the independent sector runs on churn, the chain sector on continuity.
  • Brand ledger: CVS +119 (consistent with absorbing Target's 129 in-store pharmacies after the December 2015 acquisition - TARGET-branded sites went 129 → 0), H-E-B +79, Costco +22, Genoa (behavioral-health clinic pharmacies, UnitedHealth) 2 → 38; Brookshire -53, Randalls -19, and Medicine Shoppe franchises collapsed 27 → 6.

Why the registries miss this

National pharmacy datasets used in research and policy are annual snapshots or self-reported: NCPDP's pharmacy database, files derived from the NPI registry (NPPES), Medicare pharmacy characteristics files. Researchers working from them have had to infer closure - "last year reported as active" (Guadamuz et al., Health Affairs 2025) - or, working from a state license roster directly, to bucket closures into two-year groups because the roster carries no closure date (Massachusetts Health Policy Commission, October 2025). NPPES keeps a closed pharmacy "Active" until someone deactivates the NPI, which often never happens: in spot checks of pharmacies that left the Texas roster, every one was still active in NPPES. The board roster is the regulator-primary source, and it publishes no closure date either - the license is deleted. Recording the roster daily is the only way to observe the closure on the day it happens, keyed to the license number the regulator itself uses.

Method and limits

  • Source: 75 raw Internet Archive captures of phydsk.csv, digest-deduplicated to 49 distinct rosters (2014-11-14 → 2025-07-01), plus the live capture of 2026-08-08. Reproducible from the archive.
  • Join key is the board's license number. A pharmacy that closed and re-licensed under a new number counts as one removal plus one new license, not a survival.
  • "Removal" means removal from the licensure roster. Most are business closures; relocations that trigger re-licensure look the same in this series. The daily panel recorded from 2026 forward carries address and status fields and can separate the two.
  • Chain/independent classification is by name and is imperfect: small regional chains classify as independent, and a pharmacy renamed by an acquirer attributes its later fate to the acquirer's brand.
  • Independent corroboration: the 13-month gap between the last archive capture and the first live capture shows exactly 421 removals and 451 new licenses by this method - the same figures derived separately by a different pipeline.
